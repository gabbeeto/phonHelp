-What is phonetics?
Phonetics is the study of how sounds are produced, transmitted and perceived. It deals with the physical properties of speech sounds.
    Focuses on the production of sounds
    A descriptive tool necessary for studying phonological aspects of a language
    About the sounds of language

-What is phonology?
Phonology is the study of how sounds function in relation to each other in a language.
    About the sound systems of language
    Studies how sounds pattern and interact
    Different from phonetics: phonetics = sounds of language, phonology = sound systems of language





Bilabial sounds are produced when the lips are brought together.
English sounds:
	/p/ - voiceless (as in pay)
	/b/ - voiced (as in bay)
	/m/ - voiced (as in may)
Labiodental sounds are made when the lower lip is raised towards the upper front teeth.
English sounds:
	/f/ - voiceless (as in safe)
	/v/ - voiced (as in save)
Dental sounds are produced by touching the upper front teeth with the tip of the tongue.
English sounds:
    /θ/ - voiceless (as in oath)
    /ð/ - voiced (as in clothe)
Alveolar sounds are made by raising the tip of the tongue towards the alveolar ridge (the ridge right behind the upper front teeth).
English sounds:
    /t, s/ - voiceless (as in too, sue)
    /d, z, n, l, r/ - voiced (as in do, zoo, nook, look, rook)
Palatoalveolar sounds are made by raising the blade of the tongue towards the part of the palate just behind the alveolar ridge.
English sounds:
    /ʃ, tʃ/ - voiceless (as in pressure, batch)
    /ʒ, dʒ/ - voiced (as in pleasure, badge)
Palatal sounds are produced further back towards the velum (similar to palatoalveolar).
English sounds:
    /j/ - voiced (as in yes, yellow, beauty, new)
    This is the only palatal sound in English
Velar sounds are made by raising the back of the tongue towards the soft palate (velum) .
English sounds:
    /k/ - voiceless (as in back)
    /g, ŋ/ - voiced (as in bag, bang)
    /w/ - velar with lip rounding (as in why)
Glottal sounds are produced when the air passes through the glottis (the space between the vocal folds) as it is narrowed.
English sounds:
    /h/ - (as in high)
    Also the glottal stop /ʔ/ (as in football pronounced with a complete closure of the glottis instead of /t/: [fu?bɔːl])







Plosives are sounds in which there is a complete closure in the mouth, air is blocked for a fraction of a second, then released with a small burst of sound (plosion).
English plosives:
    Bilabial: /p/ (voiceless), /b/ (voiced) - park, bark
    Alveolar: /t/ (voiceless), /d/ (voiced) - tar, dark
    Velar: /k/ (voiceless), /g/ (voiced) - car, guard

Fricatives have a closure which is not quite complete. The air is not blocked but there is enough obstruction to create friction noise.
English fricatives:
    Labiodental: /f/ (voiceless), /v/ (voiced) - wife, wives
    Dental: /θ/ (voiceless), /ð/ (voiced) - breath, breathe
    Alveolar: /s/ (voiceless), /z/ (voiced) - sink, zinc
    Palatoalveolar: /ʃ/ (voiceless), /ʒ/ (voiced) - nation, evasion
    Glottal: /h/ - help

Affricates are a combination of a plosive and a fricative. They begin with a complete closure (like plosives) but have a slow release, moving to a place where friction can be heard.
English affricates (both palatoalveolar):
    /tʃ/ - voiceless (as in chin, rich)
    /dʒ/ - voiced (as in gin, ridge)

Nasals resemble plosives but the velum is lowered, allowing air to escape through the nasal cavity while there is complete closure in the mouth.
English nasals (all voiced):
    /m/ - Bilabial (as in ram)
    /n/ - Alveolar (as in ran)
    /ŋ/ - Velar (as in rang)

Laterals are sounds where the air escapes around the sides of the tongue.
English lateral:
    /l/ - Voiced alveolar lateral
    Has two versions:
        "Clear l" - before vowels (as in light, long) - top of tongue raised
        "Dark l" - in other positions (as in milk, ball) - back of tongue raised
        Written as [ɫ] for dark l

Approximants are sounds where the tongue only approaches the roof of the mouth, not enough obstruction to create friction. All are voiced.
English approximants:
    /r/ - Alveolar/Post-alveolar (as in right, brown)
    /j/ - Palatal (as in use, youth)
    /w/ - Velar/Labio-velar with lip rounding (as in why, twin, square)
	Note: /r/ only occurs before vowels in Southern British English




Vowel features:
Vowels are classified according to:
    Tongue height – how high in the mouth the tongue is
		High (Close): Tongue is raised close to the roof of the mouth
		    Examples: /iː/ (see), /ɪ/ (sit), /uː/ (too), /ʊ/ (put)
		Mid: Tongue is halfway between high and low
		    Examples: /e/ (bed), /ə/ (banana), /ɜː/ (girl), /ɔː/ (door)
		Low (Open): Tongue is lowered, mouth is more open
			Examples: /æ/ (cat), /ʌ/ (cup), /ɑː/ (car), /ɒ/ (clock)
    Tongue backness – which part of the tongue is the highest (front, central, or back)
			Front vowels: Tongue is raised towards the front of the mouth
			    Examples: /iː/ (see), /ɪ/ (sit), /e/ (bed), /æ/ (cat)
			Central vowels: Tongue is in the middle of the mouth
			    Examples: /ɜː/ (girl), /ə/ (banana), /ʌ/ (cup)
			Back vowels: Tongue is raised towards the back of the mouth
			    Examples: /uː/ (too), /ʊ/ (put), /ɔː/ (door), /ɒ/ (clock), /ɑː/ (car)
    Lip rounding – whether the lips are rounded or unrounded
		Rounded vowels: Lips are pushed forward and rounded (like making an "o" shape)
		    Examples: /uː/ (too), /ʊ/ (put), /ɔː/ (door), /ɒ/ (clock)
		Unrounded vowels: Lips are spread or neutral (not rounded)
		    Examples: /iː/ (see), /ɪ/ (sit), /e/ (bed), /æ/ (cat), /ɑː/ (car), /ʌ/ (cup), /ɜː/ (girl), /ə/ (banana)
		Key pattern: All front and central vowels in English are unrounded; only back vowels can be rounded (though /ɑː/ is back but unrounded).
    Length – whether the vowel is long or short
		Long vowels (indicated by /ː/ in transcription):
		    /iː/ – see
		    /ɜː/ – girl
		    /ɑː/ – car
		    /ɔː/ – door
		    /uː/ – too
		Short vowels (no length mark):
		    /ɪ/ – sit
		    /e/ – bed
		    /æ/ – cat
		    /ʌ/ – cup
		    /ɒ/ – clock
		    /ʊ/ – put
		    /ə/ – banana
		Note: Length is not just about duration – long and short vowels also differ in quality (tongue position).
    Monophthong vs Diphthong – whether the vowel quality is steady or changes
		Monophthong (Pure Vowel):
		    A vowel with a steady, unchanging quality throughout its production
		    The tongue position remains fixed
		    Examples: /iː/, /e/, /ɑː/, /ʌ/, /uː/ etc.
		Diphthong (Gliding Vowel):
		    A vowel where the quality changes during its production
		    The tongue glides from one position to another within the same syllable
		    Examples: /eɪ/ (take), /aɪ/ (five), /əʊ/ (no), /eə/ (care)
