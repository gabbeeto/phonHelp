Phonetics vs. Phonology

Phonetics — the study of how sounds are produced, transmitted and perceived. It deals with the physical properties of speech sounds.

  • Focuses on the production of sounds
  • A descriptive tool for studying phonological aspects
  • About the sounds of language

Phonology — the study of how sounds function in relation to each other in a language.

  • About the sound systems of language
  • Studies how sounds pattern and interact
  • Different from phonetics: phonetics = sounds, phonology = sound systems
Rule of thumb: phonetics = the sounds themselves; phonology = the system those sounds belong to.

Consonants vs Vowels

A consonant is a speech sound produced with an obstruction or narrowing somewhere in the vocal tract (lips, teeth, tongue, palate, velum, glottis); the airflow is blocked, partly blocked, or made turbulent.
A vowel is a speech sound produced with no significant obstruction — air flows freely through the mouth, and the sound is shaped mainly by tongue height/backness, lip rounding and length. Consonants typically sit at the edges of a syllable; vowels typically form its centre. All vowels in English are voiced

Syllable division(don't memorize this for the test)

A syllable consists of a vowel sound, or a vowel sound + consonant(s). The system for dividing words into syllables is generally a phonetic one, but since English spelling is a poor guide to sound (see Transcription hints), a few practical rules help. Most words have the same number of syllables in the written form as in the pronunciation — but not all of them.

The rules

a) One vowel sound per syllable.

When a consonant separates two vowels, divide after the first vowel and before the consonant:

stu-dent · re-sult · ex-a-mine

b) Open syllable — vowel at the end of the syllable.

The vowel has its long sound:

may · be-low · an-ec-dote

c) Closed syllable — vowel not at the end of the syllable.

The vowel has its short sound:

mad · sub-ject · con-vent

d) Doubled consonants.

Divide between them — unless the doubled consonant belongs to a base word:

din-ner · swim-ming · tell-er

e) Monosyllabic prefixes and suffixes are not divided.

il-le-gal · un-com-mon · gov-ern-ment · cou-ra-geous

f) Plurisyllabic prefixes and suffixes are divided.

an-ti-war · un-der-take · vel-o-ci-ty · hy-po-the-ti-cal

Open vs. closed syllables

  • open syllable ends in a vowel — long / tense quality — may, be-, glo-
  • closed syllable ends in a consonant — short / lax quality — mad, sub-, con-
Careful: “long” and “short” here are the spelling names for the vowel letters. In phonetic terms what matters is that an open syllable has no coda (the vowel is the last thing in the syllable) and a closed syllable does. Compare hop /hɒp/ vs. hope /həʊp/, bit /bɪt/ vs. bite /baɪt/.

Worked examples

  • mirror mir-ror /ˈmɪr.ə/ — rule d
  • sunshine sun-shine /ˈsʌn.ʃaɪn/ — compound
  • poem po-em /ˈpəʊ.ɪm/ — rule a
  • wonderful won-der-ful /ˈwʌn.də.fl̩/ — rules a + e
  • calendar cal-en-dar /ˈkæl.ən.də/ — rule a
  • global glo-bal /ˈɡləʊ.bl̩/ — rule b (open)
  • fitness fit-ness /ˈfɪt.nəs/ — rule e
  • December De-cem-ber /dɪˈsem.bə/ — rule a
  • computer com-pu-ter /kəmˈpjuː.tə/ — rule a
  • lovely love-ly /ˈlʌv.li/ — rule e
  • oversleep o-ver-sleep /ˌəʊ.vəˈsliːp/ — rule f
  • dinner din-ner /ˈdɪn.ə/ — rule d
  • antidote an-ti-dote /ˈæn.tɪ.dəʊt/ — rule f
  • fine fine /faɪn/ — one syllable, cannot be divided
  • tongue tongue /tʌŋ/ — one syllable, cannot be divided

Two notations

  • - hyphen = spelling-based division, as in a dictionary entry or a worksheet — din-ner
  • . full stop = phonetic syllable boundary in a transcription — /ˈdɪn.ə/
Where the two disagree: written syllables and spoken syllables can differ. chocolate is written with 3–4 “vowels” but spoken /ˈtʃɒk.lət/ — 2 syllables; every is often /ˈev.ri/; business is /ˈbɪz.nəs/. Always divide the sound, not the letters.
Link to stress: once the syllables are cut, the stress mark ˈ goes at the start of the strongest one — cal-en-dar → /ˈkæl.ən.də/, com-pu-ter → /kəmˈpjuː.tə/. See Syllables & the stress mark.

Consonants · Place of Articulation

The place of articulation is the point in the vocal tract where the airflow is obstructed when producing a consonant sound.

Bilabial

Both lips brought together.

  • /p/ voiceless pay
  • /b/ voiced bay
  • /m/ voiced may

Labiodental

Lower lip raised towards the upper front teeth.

  • /f/ voiceless safe
  • /v/ voiced save

Dental

Tip of the tongue touches the upper front teeth.

  • /θ/ voiceless oath
  • /ð/ voiced clothe

Alveolar

Tip of the tongue towards the alveolar ridge.

  • /t/ voiceless too
  • /s/ voiceless sue
  • /d/ voiced do
  • /z/ voiced zoo
  • /n/ voiced nook
  • /l/ voiced look
  • /r/ voiced rook

Palato-alveolar

Blade of the tongue towards the palate behind the alveolar ridge.

  • /ʃ/ voiceless pressure
  • /tʃ/ voiceless batch
  • /ʒ/ voiced pleasure
  • /dʒ/ voiced badge

Palatal

Further back towards the velum.

  • /j/ voiced yes, yellow, beauty, new
The only palatal sound in English.

Velar

Back of the tongue towards the soft palate (velum).

  • /k/ voiceless back
  • /g/ voiced bag
  • /ŋ/ voiced bang
  • /w/ voiced why (velar with lip rounding)

Glottal

Air passes through the narrowed glottis.

  • /h/ high

Consonants · Manner of Articulation

The manner of articulation is how the airflow is obstructed when producing a consonant sound.

Plosives

Complete closure, air blocked, released with a sudden burst of air.

  • /p/ voiceless bilabial — park
  • /b/ voiced bilabial — bark
  • /t/ voiceless alveolar — tar
  • /d/ voiced alveolar — dark
  • /k/ voiceless velar — car
  • /g/ voiced velar — guard

Fricatives

Near-closure creates friction noise.

  • /f/ voiceless labiodental — wife
  • /v/ voiced labiodental — wives
  • /θ/ voiceless dental — breath
  • /ð/ voiced dental — breathe
  • /s/ voiceless alveolar — sink
  • /z/ voiced alveolar — zinc
  • /ʃ/ voiceless palato-alveolar — nation
  • /ʒ/ voiced palato-alveolar — evasion
  • /h/ glottal — help

Affricates

Plosive + fricative(stopped like plosive but released slowly like fricative instead of released with a sudden burst of air)

  • /tʃ/ voiceless palato-alveolar — chin, rich
  • /dʒ/ voiced palato-alveolar — gin, ridge

Nasals

Complete closure, velum lowered, air escapes through the nose.

  • /m/ voiced bilabial — ram
  • /n/ voiced alveolar — ran
  • /ŋ/ voiced velar — rang

Laterals

Air escapes around the sides of the tongue.

  • /l/ voiced alveolar lateral
English /l/ has two main realisations — clear l [l] and dark l [ɫ]. See Clear l & Dark l.

Approximants

Made by moving parts of your mouth close together but not close enough to make a friction so the air flows smoothly.

  • /r/ alveolar / post-alveolar — right, brown
  • /j/ palatal — use, youth
  • /w/ velar / labio-velar with lip rounding — why, twin, square
/r/ only occurs before vowels in Southern British English.

Voiced vs. Voiceless Consonants

Every consonant is either voiced or voiceless (also called unvoiced). The difference is what the vocal cords do while the sound is made — the place and manner of articulation stay the same.

  • Voiced — the vocal cords vibrate as air passes between them. Put two fingers on your throat and say zzz — you feel a buzz. In English: /b, d, g, v, ð, z, ʒ, dʒ, m, n, ŋ, l, r, j, w/.
  • Voiceless — the vocal cords are held apart and do not vibrate; only air and friction noise. Say sss — no buzz. In English: /p, t, k, f, θ, s, ʃ, tʃ, h/ (plus the glottal stop /ʔ/).

Voicing pairs

Most voiceless consonants have a voiced twin — same place and manner, only voicing differs.

  • /p/ – /b/ voiceless voiced — pin / bin
  • /t/ – /d/ voiceless voiced — tin / din
  • /k/ – /g/ voiceless voiced — coat / goat
  • /f/ – /v/ voiceless voiced — fan / van
  • /θ/ – /ð/ voiceless voiced — thigh / thy
  • /s/ – /z/ voiceless voiced — sip / zip
  • /ʃ/ – /ʒ/ voiceless voiced — pressure / pleasure
  • /tʃ/ – /dʒ/ voiceless voiced — chin / gin
Quick test: fingers on your throat, say ssss then zzzz — the buzz appears only for the voiced one. With ears covered, voiceless sounds feel “hissy” and voiced sounds feel “buzzy”.

Clear l & Dark l

/l/ is a voiced alveolar lateral approximant: the tip of the tongue touches the alveolar ridge while the air escapes around the sides of the tongue. English has two main realisations of /l/, and which one you use depends on where the /l/ stands in the syllable.

Clear l [l]

Used before a vowel (onset position). The front of the tongue is raised towards the hard palate, which gives a “light”, fronted resonance. It is the “normal” l of light.

  • light /laɪt/
  • long /lɒŋ/
  • leave /liːv/
  • lovely /ˈlʌvli/
  • play /pleɪ/ · clean /kliːn/
  • yellow /ˈjeləʊ/ · million /ˈmɪljən/

Dark l [ɫ]

Used before a consonant and word-finally (coda position). The back of the tongue is raised towards the velum — the sound is velarized — which gives a darker, heavier resonance. The phonetic symbol for it is [ɫ], though in broad transcription it is still written /l/.

  • milk /mɪlk/ → [mɪɫk]
  • ball /bɔːl/ → [bɔːɫ]
  • feel /fiːl/ → [fiːɫ]
  • help /help/ → [heɫp]
  • cold /kəʊld/ → [kəʊɫd]
  • table /ˈteɪbl̩/ — syllabic l

Summary

  • Clear l [l] — before a vowel: light, long, play, lovely.
  • Dark l [ɫ] — before a consonant or at the end of a word: milk, help, ball, feel.
Accent note: in Estuary English / Cockney and in Australian English the dark l may vocalise into a vowel or /w/ — milk [mɪʊk], ball [bɔːw].

Vowels

Vowels are classified by tongue height, tongue backness, lip rounding and length.
All vowels are voiced so it's not classified by voicing(unless you're whispering then all vowels are voiceless).

Tongue height

  • High (close): tongue raised close to the roof of the mouth — /iː/ see, /ɪ/ sit, /uː/ too, /ʊ/ put
  • Mid: tongue halfway — /e/ bed, /ə/ banana, /ɜː/ girl, /ɔː/ door
  • Low (open): tongue lowered, mouth more open — /æ/ cat, /ʌ/ cup, /ɑː/ car, /ɒ/ clock

Tongue backness

  • Front: /iː/ see, /ɪ/ sit, /e/ bed, /æ/ cat
  • Central: /ɜː/ girl, /ə/ banana, /ʌ/ cup
  • Back: /uː/ too, /ʊ/ put, /ɔː/ door, /ɒ/ clock, /ɑː/ car

Lip rounding

  • Rounded: /uː/, /ʊ/, /ɔː/, /ɒ/ — lips pushed forward
  • Unrounded: all front & central vowels; also /ɑː/ (back but unrounded)

Length

long /iː/ see
short /ɪ/ sit
long /ɜː/ girl
short /e/ bed
long /ɑː/ car
short /æ/ cat
long /ɔː/ door
short /ʌ/ cup
long /uː/ too
short /ɒ/ clock
short /ʊ/ put
short /ə/ banana
Long vowels (/ː/) differ not only in duration but also in quality.

Monophthong vs. Diphthong

  • Monophthong — steady quality (one vowel): /iː/, /e/, /ɑː/, /ʌ/, /uː/, etc.
  • Diphthong — gliding quality (a movement from one vowel to another): /eɪ/ take, /aɪ/ five, /əʊ/ no, /eə/ care

The suffix -s / -es (plural nouns & 3rd person singular)

The written suffix -s / -es is pronounced in one of three ways, depending on the final sound of the base word (never on its spelling). The same rule applies to plural regular nouns and to third-person singular verbs in the present tense.

/ɪz/ — after sibilants

Added when the base ends in a hissing or hushing sound: /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/. This adds a whole extra syllable.

/ɪz/ after /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/

kiss → kisses /kɪs/ → /ˈkɪsɪz/

lose → loses /luːz/ → /ˈluːzɪz/

wish → wishes /wɪʃ/ → /ˈwɪʃɪz/

message → messages /ˈmesɪdʒ/ → /ˈmesɪdʒɪz/

teach → teaches /tiːtʃ/ → /ˈtiːtʃɪz/

judge → judges /dʒʌdʒ/ → /ˈdʒʌdʒɪz/

/s/ — after voiceless sounds

Added when the base ends in a voiceless consonant (other than the sibilants above): /p, t, k, f, θ/.

/s/ after /p, t, k, f, θ/

hit → hits /hɪt/ → /hɪts/

cough → coughs /kɒf/ → /kɒfs/

stop → stops /stɒp/ → /stɒps/

laugh → laughs /lɑːf/ → /lɑːfs/

bath → baths /bɑːθ/ → /bɑːθs/

/z/ — everywhere else

Added after all voiced sounds — voiced consonants /b, d, g, v, ð, m, n, ŋ, l, r/ and all vowels (vowels are voiced).

/z/ after voiced sounds & vowels

go → goes /ɡəʊ/ → /ɡəʊz/

love → loves /lʌv/ → /lʌvz/

run → runs /rʌn/ → /rʌnz/

play → plays /pleɪ/ → /pleɪz/

read → reads /riːd/ → /riːdz/

Spelling ≠ sound: -es is written after sibilants, but the pronunciation is what decides. goes ends in a vowel sound → /ɡəʊz/; kisses ends in /s/ → /ˈkɪsɪz/.
Why: this is voicing assimilation. The suffix copies the voicing of the sound before it — voiceless /s/ after voiceless sounds, voiced /z/ after voiced ones. /ɪz/ exists only to break up two sibilants, which would otherwise be impossible to pronounce distinctly.

The suffix -ed / -d (regular past tense)

The written ending -ed also has three pronunciations, again decided by the final sound of the base verb. Only one of the three adds a syllable.

/ɪd/ — after /t/ or /d/

Added when the verb already ends in an alveolar plosive, /t/ or /d/. This adds a whole extra syllable.

/ɪd/ after /t, d/

need → needed /niːd/ → /ˈniːdɪd/

wait → waited /weɪt/ → /ˈweɪtɪd/

want → wanted /wɒnt/ → /ˈwɒntɪd/

start → started /stɑːt/ → /ˈstɑːtɪd/

decide → decided /dɪˈsaɪd/ → /dɪˈsaɪdɪd/

/t/ — after voiceless sounds

Added when the verb ends in a voiceless consonant (other than /t/): /p, k, f, θ, s, ʃ, tʃ/.

/t/ after /p, k, f, θ, s, ʃ, tʃ/

stop → stopped /stɒp/ → /stɒpt/

like → liked /laɪk/ → /laɪkt/

kiss → kissed /kɪs/ → /kɪst/

watch → watched /wɒtʃ/ → /wɒtʃt/

laugh → laughed /lɑːf/ → /lɑːft/

breathe → breathed /briːð/ → /briːðd/

/d/ — everywhere else

Added after all voiced sounds (other than /d/) — voiced consonants /b, g, v, ð, z, ʒ, dʒ, m, n, ŋ, l, r/ and all vowels.

/d/ after voiced sounds & vowels

play → played /pleɪ/ → /pleɪd/

live → lived /lɪv/ → /lɪvd/

call → called /kɔːl/ → /kɔːld/

rob → robbed /rɒb/ → /rɒbd/

open → opened /ˈəʊpən/ → /ˈəʊpənd/

agree → agreed /əˈɡriː/ → /əˈɡriːd/

Spelling ≠ sound: the letter e in -ed is usually silent — liked /laɪkt/ is one syllable, not two. Only /ɪd/ adds a syllable.
Why: again voicing assimilation. /t/ is voiceless so it follows voiceless sounds; /d/ is voiced so it follows voiced sounds. /ɪd/ is used when the base already ends in /t/ or /d/, because adding another identical plosive would give an unpronounceable sequence.

Function words vs. Content words

Content words (also called lexical words) carry the core meaning of the sentence — the “dictionary” information. They are an open class: new ones are created all the time (internet, selfie, download). Nouns, main verbs, adjectives and adverbs belong here. They are normally stressed and keep their full vowel sounds.

Function words (also called grammatical words) carry very little meaning on their own; they exist to show the grammatical relationships between the content words. They are a closed class: articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliaries, conjunctions, determiners and articles. They are normally unstressed and appear in weak forms with a reduced vowel (usually /ə/ or /ɪ/).

Examples

  • Content:
    table /ˈteɪbl̩/
    running /ˈrʌnɪŋ/
    beautiful /ˈbjuːtɪfl̩/
    quickly /ˈkwɪkli/
  • Function (strong → weak):
    and /ænd/ → /ənd/ → /ən/
    of /ɒv/ → /əv/
    for /fɔː/ → /fə/
    to /tuː/ → /tə/
    have /hæv/ → /əv/
    but /bʌt/ → /bət/

Main differences

  • Meaning: content = lexical meaning; function = grammatical meaning.
  • Class: content = open (new words appear); function = closed (fixed list).
  • Stress: content words are usually stressed; function words are usually unstressed.
  • Vowels: content words keep full vowels; function words reduce to weak forms.
  • Connected speech: function words are the ones most affected by assimilation and linking (e.g. an illuminati → /ənɪluːmɪnˈɑːti/).
A function word can take stress when it is contrasted: “I said give it to me, not from me.”

Citation form vs. Connected speech

Citation form is the isolated pronunciation of a word — the way it appears in a dictionary. It is produced slowly, carefully and with clear articulation, with the word's full (strong) form and full vowels, and with no influence from neighbouring sounds.

Connected speech is how words are actually pronounced in everyday, natural conversation. Words are not produced one by one; they form a continuous sequence of sounds in which each sound is affected by the sounds around it. This produces:

  • Assimilation — ten players /ten ˈpleɪəz/ → /tem ˈpleɪəz/
  • Linking — an apple /əˈnæpl̩/
  • Weak forms — and /ænd/ → /ən/
  • Gemination — black cat /blækːæt/
In one sentence: citation form is one word alone, slow and complete; connected speech is many words together, fast and adapted to each other.

Linking

Linking (also called liaison) is the smooth joining of the final consonant of one word to the initial vowel of the next word, inside the same thought group. Instead of stopping after each word, the speaker pronounces the final consonant as if it began the following word, so the speech flows continuously.

The pattern is: consonant + vowel (C + V) across a word boundary.

  • an apple /ən ˈæpl̩/ → /əˈnæpl̩/
  • turn it off /tɜːn ɪt ɒf/ → /ˈtɜːnɪtɒf/
  • pick it up /pɪk ɪt ʌp/ → /pɪkɪtʌp/
  • not at all /nɒt ət ɔːl/ → /nɒtətɔːl/
  • come in /kʌm ɪn/ → /kʌˈmɪn/
  • find out /faɪnd aʊt/ → /faɪnˈdaʊt/
  • take it easy /teɪk ɪt ˈiːzi/ → /teɪkɪtˈiːzi/
  • leave it alone /liːv ɪt əˈləʊn/ → /liːvɪtəˈləʊn/
Linking is one of the “smoothing” processes of connected speech, together with assimilation (sounds change).

Gemination

Gemination (also called twinning or doubling) happens when the same consonant sound ends one word and begins the next one. Instead of pronouncing two separate consonants, the speaker holds the articulation once and lengthens it — the two sounds merge into one long consonant.

  • black cat /blæk kæt/ → /blækːæt/
  • big game /bɪg geɪm/ → /bɪgːeɪm/
  • hot tea /hɒt tiː/ → /hɒtːiː/
  • good day /gʊd deɪ/ → /gʊdːeɪ/
  • some money /sʌm ˈmʌni/ → /sʌmːʌni/
  • ten nails /ten neɪlz/ → /tenːeɪlz/
  • this Saturday /ðɪs ˈsætədeɪ/ → /ðɪsːætədeɪ/
  • book case /bʊk keɪs/ → /bʊkːeɪs/
  • night time /naɪt taɪm/ → /naɪtːaɪm/

Assimilation

Assimilation is the process by which a sound changes in order to become more like a neighbouring sound. It makes speech smoother and easier to pronounce, because the articulators do not have to move as far or as fast between two positions. The change is usually in the place of articulation but in the case of palato-alveolarization when the previous word ends either in /d/ or /t/, there's a change in the manner of articulation(from plosive to affricate).

Bilabialization

/d/ + /p,m,b/ = /b/ bad men /bæd men/ → /bæb men/

good morning /gʊd ˈmɔːnɪŋ/ → /gʊb ˈmɔːnɪŋ/

red pen /red pen/ → /reb pen/

good boy /gʊd bɔɪ/ → /gʊb bɔɪ/

handbag /ˈhændbæg/ → /ˈhæmbæg/

/t/ + /p,m,b/ = /p/ that man /ðæt mæn/ → /ðæp mæn/

that boy /ðæt bɔɪ/ → /ðæp bɔɪ/

Linking R

The word is spelled with an “r” (or “re”) at the end — far, four, here, better, mother. Said alone, or before a consonant, the /r/ is silent. But when the next word begins with a vowel, that /r/ is pronounced and “links” the two words together. The /r/ was always historically there; it is simply kept.

  • far away /fɑː əˈweɪ/ → /fɑːr əˈweɪ/
  • four apples /fɔː ˈæpl̩z/ → /fɔːr ˈæpl̩z/
  • here and there /hɪə ənd ðeə/ → /hɪər ənd ðeə/
  • better and better /ˈbetə ənd ˈbetə/ → /ˈbetər ənd ˈbetə/

Transcription hints

Practical tips for making transcriptions, so you make sure you don’t mess up:

  1. Use / / for broad (phonemic) and [ ] for narrow (allophonic) transcription.
  2. Always mark primary stress ˈ before the stressed syllable.
  3. Transcribe what you hear, not the spelling (for example ‘going to’ = /ˈɡəʊɪŋ tə/).
  4. There are no capital letters, commas, full stops/periods, question marks in transcriptions.
  5. The symbol | is used to mark a point where a speaker of the text might introduce a brief pause. Oftentimes this coincides with a place where a punctuation mark is present on the text, but not always.
  6. * is used to indicate that the following word is a name.
  7. Many words in connected speech are pronounced (and should be transcribed) differently in different environments.
  8. Be careful to think about how speech sounds and to avoid being misled by the spelling.
  9. Sometimes the ordinary spelling version uses letters which do not correspond to any sound at all, so they shouldn’t appear in transcription.
Quick reference:
/ / = broad (phonemic)
[ ] = narrow (allophonic)
ˈ = primary stress
| = brief pause
* = the next word is a name.

Escrita: Grammar · Conjunctions (and, but, or, so, because)

We use these words (conjunctions) to join two sentences. They make one longer sentence from two shorter sentences.

Sentence A + Sentence B

The car stopped. + The driver got out.

→ The car stopped and the driver got out.

and / but / or

  • and — adds information or joins similar actions/things.
    I got home, had something to eat, sat down in an armchair and fell asleep.
    Ann is at work, Sue has gone shopping and Chris is playing football.
  • but — shows a contrast or opposing idea.
    My sister is married but she lives in London.
    He doesn't like her but she doesn't like him.
  • or — shows a choice between two things.
    Do you want to go out or are you too tired?

so (the result of something)

Use so when the second sentence is the result of the first.

Result

It was very hot, so I opened the window.

The water wasn't clean, so we didn't go swimming.

They like films, so they often go to the cinema.

because (the reason for something)

Use because when the second sentence is the reason for the first.

Reason

I opened the window because it was very hot.

We didn't go swimming because the water wasn't clean.

Lisa is hungry because she didn't have breakfast.

Position of 'because': Because is also possible at the beginning of the sentence.
Because the water wasn't clean, we didn't go swimming.

More than one conjunction

In these examples there is more than one conjunction:

  • It was late and I was tired, so I went to bed.
  • I always enjoy visiting London, but I wouldn't like to live there because it's too big.

Unit 1: am/is/are (Positive and Negative)

The verb be has three forms in the present tense: am, is, and are. We often use short forms (contractions) in spoken and informal English.

Positive
I am (I'm)
he / she / it is (he's / she's / it's)
we / you / they are (we're / you're / they're)
Negative
I am not (I'm not)
he / she / it is not (he's not / he isn't)
we / you / they are not (we're not / you aren't)

Examples

I'm cold. Can you close the window, please?

My brother is very tall. He's a policeman.

Ann and I are good friends.

I'm tired but I'm not hungry.

Jane isn't at home at the moment. She's at work.

Those people aren't English. They're Australian.

Contractions with 'that', 'there', and 'here':
that's = that is (That's very kind of you.)
there's = there is (Look! There's Chris.)
here's = here is ('Here's your key.' 'Thank you.')

Unit 2: am/is/are (Questions)

To make a question, we put am, is, or are before the subject.

Question Form

Am I late? — No, you're on time.

Is your mother at home? — No, she's out.

Are your parents at home? — No, they're out.

Is it cold in your room? — Yes, a little.

Word Order: The subject comes after the verb.
Is she at home? (NOT Is at home she?)
Are your shoes new? (NOT Are new your shoes?)

Wh- Questions

We can also use question words like Where, What, Who, How, Why.

Examples

Where is your mother? Is she at home?

What colour is your car? — It's red.

How are your parents? Are they well?

How much are these postcards? — Fifty pence.

Common contractions: what's (what is), who's (who is), how's (how is), where's (where is).

When answering yes/no questions with be, we usually use a short answer. We do not use contractions in positive short answers.

Yes, ...
Yes, I am.
Yes, he/she/it is.
Yes, we/you/they are.
No, ...
No, I'm not.

Escrita: Grammar · Present Time

Present Simple: Form

Form the present simple with the infinitive form of the verb (without to). Add -s in the third person singular he/she/it.

I like
You like
He/She/It likes
We like
They like
Irregular verbs: have and be.
I have / You have / He/She/It has / We have / They have
I am / You are / He/She/It is / We are / They are

Spelling changes: Verbs ending in -o, -s, -ch, -sh, -x add -es for the third person singular.

  • He goes · She misses · She watches · He wishes · He relaxes

Present Simple: Questions & Negatives

Questions: Form yes/no questions with do + the infinitive (without to). Use does for he/she/it.

  • Do you like Italian food?
  • Does she like Italian food?
  • What do you want?
  • Where does she live?

Negatives: Form negatives with do not + the infinitive. Use does not for he/she/it.

  • I do not like that. → I don't like that.
  • She does not like that. → She doesn't like that.

Present Simple: Meaning & Pronunciation

The present simple can describe:

  1. Habitual actions. I usually get up at 7.30.
  2. Actions and situations that are generally or usually true. Liz plays in the school basketball team.
  3. Facts which are always true. The sun rises in the east.

Pronunciation: Verbs which end in /z/, /dʒ/, /s/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/ and /ks/ have an extra syllable in the third person, pronounced /ɪz/.

  • watches /ˈwɒtʃɪz/ · misses /ˈmɪsɪz/ · relaxes /rɪˈlæksɪz/
  • After /f/, /k/, /p/, /t/, third person sound is /s/: hits /hɪts/
  • Other third person -s endings are pronounced as /z/: sees /siːz/

Present Continuous: Form

Form the present continuous with the auxiliary be and the -ing form of the main verb.

  • I am relaxing. / You are relaxing. / He/She is relaxing.
  • We are relaxing. / They are relaxing.

Spelling changes:

  • Verbs ending -e drop the -e when they add -ing. (like → liking, decide → deciding, write → writing)
  • Verbs with one syllable, ending in one vowel and one consonant, double the consonant. (sit → sitting, swim → swimming, dig → digging)
  • Verbs ending -ie change -ie to -y. (lie → lying, tie → tying, die → dying)

Questions: Put the auxiliary be before the subject.

  • Are you writing?
  • Is he writing?
  • What are you writing?

Negatives: Use the verb be + not.

  • I am not writing. / I 'm not writing.
  • He is not writing. / He isn't writing.
  • They are not writing. / They aren't writing.

Present Continuous: Meaning

Use the present continuous to describe:

  1. Actions in progress at the present moment. Sorry, I can't speak to you, I'm washing my hair.
  2. Actions happening 'around now', but not at the moment of speaking. I'm reading The Lord of the Rings.
  3. Future meanings (fixed future arrangements). Paul is leaving early tomorrow morning.

Frequency Adverbs

Frequency adverbs explain how often something happens. The adverb usually comes between the subject and the verb.

  • always (✓✓✓✓) — I always get up at 7.00.
  • usually (✓✓✓✓) — It usually rains when I go on holiday!
  • often (✓✓✓) — Pat often goes to football matches.
  • sometimes (✓✓) — We sometimes eat pizza for lunch.
  • rarely (✓) — Jane rarely listens to jazz.
  • never (-) — My bus never arrives on time.
With the verb be, the adverb comes after the verb.
Jim is usually late.

Present continuous with always: We can use always with the present continuous when we are exaggerating or complaining. We emphasize always in speech.

  • You're always forgetting your keys!

State Verbs vs. Action Verbs

A state is when something stays the same. An action is when something happens. State verbs are not usually used in any continuous form.

  • I know what you mean. (NOT I am knowing what you're meaning.)

Examples of state verbs:

  • Senses: appear, hear, look like, see, taste
  • Feeling: like, hate, love, prefer, want, wish
  • Thinking: agree, believe, forget, know, think, understand
  • Possession: belong to, contain, have, own
  • Being: be, exist
  • Other: cost, depend on, mean, need
State + Action meanings: Some verbs can have a 'state' meaning and an 'action' meaning. Examples include be, have, taste, think.
I have two sisters. (permanent state)
I'm having problems with this computer. (temporary action)
This cake tastes wonderful. (state)
I'm tasting the soup. (action)

Present Simple vs. Present Continuous

Present simple Present continuous
permanent temporary
habits and routines in progress now
facts that are always true events happening at the moment
general situations a particular situation
I live in Budapest. (all the time) I'm living in Budapest. (for a few months)
This plane lands in Frankfurt. (routine) We're landing. (in progress now)

Escrita: Grammar · Past Time 1

Past Simple: Form

Form the past simple of regular verbs by adding -ed to the infinitive (without to). For verbs ending in -e, only add -d.

  • I enjoyed the film.
  • I loved the music.
  • She decided to stay.
  • They opened the box.

The form is the same for I, you, he, she, it, we, they.

Spelling changes:

  • Verbs ending in consonant + -y change -y to -ie.
    try → tried, cry → cried
  • Verbs ending with one vowel and one consonant double the final consonant.
    regret → regretted, fit → fitted

Irregular verbs: There is a list of the most common irregular verbs on page 268 of the textbook. It is necessary to learn these forms.

  • eat → ate · drink → drank · wake → woke

Questions: Form questions with did + the infinitive (without to).

  • Did you enjoy the film?
  • Did you drink all the milk?
  • What did you do yesterday?
  • Why did she leave?

Negatives: Form negatives with did not + the infinitive without to. (Short form: didn't).

  • The coat did not fit me. → The coat didn't fit me.
  • Carol did not eat very much. → Carol didn't eat very much.

Past Simple: Meaning

  1. Actions and states in a completed period of time.
    I enjoyed the film we saw last night.
    We listened to some new CDs yesterday afternoon.
  2. Habitual actions in the past.
    Every day we got up early and went to the beach.

Past Continuous: Form

Form the past continuous with the past of the auxiliary be and the -ing form of the main verb.

  • I was sitting by the door. / You were laughing. / He was driving.
  • We were crying. / They were eating.

Questions: Form yes/no questions by putting the auxiliary be before the subject.

  • Was I sleeping?
  • Were you waiting?
  • What were you writing?

Negatives: Form negatives with the auxiliary be + not and the -ing form.

  • I was not listening. / I wasn't listening.
  • He was not playing. / He wasn't playing.
  • They were not looking. / They weren't looking.

Past Continuous: Meaning

  1. An action in progress in the past. We often use it to describe the background situation (what was happening) for a sudden event in the past simple (what happened).
    While I was waiting for the bus, I met Karen.
  2. Several situations in progress at the same time.
    While James was cooking, David was phoning a friend.
    The airport was full of people. Some were sleeping on benches, some were shopping, others were reading.

Time Expressions

Past Simple: ago, in September, last week, at 6.00, on Sunday, for three years, two years ago, at that time, once
Past Continuous: when or while (to mean 'during the time that')
  • While we were waiting, it started to rain.
  • I cut my finger when I was peeling the potatoes.
'When' vs. 'While': If we mean 'at the time that', then we only use when with the past simple.
She was very happy when she got her exam results.

Past Perfect: Form

Form the past perfect with the auxiliary had + the past participle (third column in verb tables).

  • I had decided.
  • She had left.
  • We had eaten.

In speech and informal writing, we use short forms (contractions): I'd decided, She'd left, We'd eaten.

Questions and negatives:

  • Had she left?
  • She had not left. → She hadn't left.

Past Perfect: Meaning

  1. One past event before another past event. Use the past perfect for the earlier event.
    Sue left at 7.00. We arrived at her house at 8.30.
    → When we arrived at Sue's house, she had left.
  2. With 'before' or 'after'. When we use before or after, we need not use the past perfect (the order is clear), but it is possible to use it.
    Sue left her house before we arrived. OR Sue had left her house before we arrived.
    We arrived at Sue's house after she left. OR We arrived at Sue's house after she had left.
Important: We do not use the past perfect just because an event happened a long time ago. We use the past simple.
The Chinese built the Great Wall over 2,000 years ago. (NOT had built)

Past Perfect: Common Uses

We often use the past perfect with verbs of thinking (think, know, be sure, realize, remember, suspect, understand, etc.) when we talk in the past about an event in the past.

  • I thought I'd seen the film before, but I hadn't.
  • David knew he'd seen her somewhere before.
  • When I got home I realized I'd lost my wallet.

Used to

Used to describes a habit or state in the past. It suggests that the action or situation is no longer true and makes a contrast with the present.

  • I used to play tennis, but now I play football. (habit)
  • I used to have long hair when I was younger. (state)
Questions and negatives: Form questions and negatives with did or did not and use use to.
Did you use to have long hair?
I didn't use to play tennis, but now I play most weekends.

Pronunciation: Used in this structure is pronounced /juːst/ (with a /t/ sound), different from the past tense of the verb use (/juːzd/).

Would

Would describes repeated actions in the past, but not states. It means the same as used to, but is more common in descriptive writing than in speech.

  • On winter days, we would sit / used to sit around the fire and tell stories. (action)
  • I used to own a motorbike. (NOT I would own a motorbike.) (state)

Past Perfect vs. Past Simple

We can use the past simple for both verbs, or we can put the event that happened first into the past perfect. The sentence type we use depends on which part of the meaning we want to emphasize. The part we emphasize most comes first.

Situation 1: Teacher arrives at 8.00, I start writing at 7.50.

1a) I started writing at 7.50, before my teacher arrived.

1b) When my teacher arrived, I had already started writing.

Situation 2: Film starts at 8.00, I arrive at 8.10.

2a) The film started at 8.00, but I arrived ten minutes late.

2b) I arrived at 8.10, but the film had already started.

Present Perfect: Form

Form the present perfect with the auxiliary verb have/has + the past participle (third column in verb tables).

  • I have decided to leave tomorrow. (regular)
  • Sue has sent an email to Lucy. (irregular)

In speech and informal writing we use short forms (contractions): I have decided → I've decided. She has sent → She's sent.

Questions and negatives: Put have/has before the subject for questions. Use not for negatives.

  • Have you decided yet?
  • Has she left yet?
  • Sue has not sent an email. → Sue hasn't sent an email.

Present Perfect: Meaning

The present perfect describes past events which are connected to the present. Use it to describe:

  1. Experiences in our life.
    Have you (ever) visited any other countries?
    Yes, I've been to Italy and France.
    Compare with past simple: Yes, I went to Italy and France in 2006.
  2. An event in the past with a result in the present.
    Helen has broken her pencil. (Result: She needs a sharpener.)
    I've hurt my foot. (Result: I can't play football.)
    Compare with past simple: I hurt my foot last week. (Time is important.)
  3. A situation that started in the past and continues until the present.
    I've lived here for ten years.
    I've often seen Jim with his dog in the park.
  4. The number of things finished so far.
    I've read 100 pages of this book. (An exact time is not mentioned.)

Time Expressions

ever / never (experiences in life)

Have you ever eaten Japanese food?

No, I've never eaten it.

yet / so far / already

Yet is used in questions and negative sentences.

So far (up to now) is used in questions and positive sentences.

Already is used in positive sentences for an action that happened before.

Have you finished this book yet?

No, I haven't finished yet. I'm on page 56.

I've read 56 pages so far.

I've already written it.

just (a very recent event)

Cathy has just phoned from the airport.

Frequency adverbs (always, often, etc.)

Peter has always loved animals. (a state)

We have often visited Spain. (a repeated event)

for and since

For describes the length of a time period.

Since describes the point when the time period started.

Tom has worked here for three months.

Tom has worked here since July 10th.

Passive: Form

Form the passive with be + a past participle. Compare the active and passive forms in these examples:

Present simple

Active: The Government builds hundreds of houses every year.

Passive: Hundreds of houses are built every year.

Present continuous

Active: The authorities are questioning two men.

Passive: Two men are being questioned by the authorities.

Present perfect

Active: We have chosen Helen as the new president.

Passive: Helen has been chosen as the new president.

Past simple

Active: The police arrested one protester.

Passive: One protester was arrested.

Will

Active: They will play the match on Wednesday evening.

Passive: The match will be played on Wednesday evening.

Movement: The object in the active form (hundreds of houses, two men, Helen) moves to the front in the passive sentence and becomes the subject.

Passive: Use

Focus on important information: Compare the emphasis:

  • (Active) The Government built hundreds of houses last year. → More emphasis on who did the action (the Government).
  • (Passive) Hundreds of houses were built by the Government last year. → More emphasis on the thing affected by the action (the number of houses).

Emphasized information usually comes at the beginning of the sentence. The passive is used a lot in formal writing, especially scientific writing, and is common in speech and writing in general.

Using by and with

  • by + agent The person or thing that does the action. — Hundreds of houses were built by the Government.
  • with + instrument The thing used to perform the action. — The windows were broken with a baseball bat.

Sentences without an agent

It is not always necessary to mention the agent. There are several reasons for this:

  1. Agent not known: Brenda's motorbike was stolen last night. (We do not know who stole it.)
  2. Agent obvious: One protester was arrested. (We know it is always the police who arrest people.)
  3. Agent unimportant: A lot of small cars are sold every year. (Who sells the cars is not important.)

Transitive and Intransitive Verbs

  • Transitive verbs have objects (e.g., help). A young man helped the old lady across the road. → The old lady was helped across the road by a young man.
  • Intransitive verbs do not have objects (e.g., sit). Diane sits near the front of the class. (Cannot be made passive.)
Rule: Only transitive verbs can be made passive. Some verbs can have both transitive and intransitive meanings (e.g., walk).

Escrita: Grammar · Relative Clauses (who / that / which)

We use relative clauses to join two sentences together, usually to give more information about a person or a thing.

Joining sentences

I met a woman. + She can speak six languages.

→ I met a woman who can speak six languages.

Jim was wearing a hat. + It was too big for him.

→ Jim was wearing a hat that was too big for him. (or which)

Who

Who is for people (not things).

A thief is a person who steals things.

Do you know anybody who can play the piano?

The man who phoned didn't give his name.

The people who work in the office are very friendly.

That

That is for things or people. You can use that for people, but who is more usual.

An aeroplane is a machine that flies.

Emma lives in a house that is 500 years old.

The people that work in the office are very friendly.

Which

Which is for things (not people).

An aeroplane is a machine which flies.

Emma lives in a house which is 500 years old.

Do not use 'which' for people:
Do you remember the woman who was playing the piano at the party? (NOT the woman which...)

Escrita: Grammar · Future

We can refer to the future in English by using will, be going to or by using present tenses. Sometimes there is little difference between will and be going to, but at other times we use them in different ways.

Will

Form:
will + infinitive without to.
Negative: won't.
Short form: 'll.

In formal English we also use shall with I and we to refer to the future. This is becoming rare.

Meaning: Use will to talk generally about future beliefs, opinions, hopes and predictions. There is usually a time expression. Add perhaps, probably or definitely to show how certain or uncertain we are.

Probably / definitely

They come after will but before won't:

She'll probably come with us tonight.

She probably won't come with us tonight.

  • In the next century, most people will probably live in big cities.
  • Perhaps it'll rain tomorrow.
  • United will definitely win tonight.

Be going to

Form: be + going + infinitive.

  • Jean is going to learn to drive.
  • Tim and Ann are going to travel abroad next year.

Meaning:

  1. Plans and intentions. The plan may be in the near future, or more distant.
    I'm going to do lots of work this evening.
    After I finish university I'm going to travel all over Europe by InterRail.
  2. Prediction with present evidence. When we can see in the present situation that something is going to happen.
    Look out! You're going to drop the plates!

Present continuous

Meaning: Use the present continuous future to talk about things we have arranged to do, such as social arrangements and appointments. There is usually a time expression.

  • A: Are you doing anything on Friday evening?
    B: Not really. Why?
    A: I'm having a party. Would you like to come?
State verbs (e.g. be, know) are not usually used in a continuous form, so we use will or going to with these verbs.
I'll be back on Friday. / I'm going to be back on Friday.

Will or going to?

We can make predictions with will and going to, and on most occasions either is possible.
I think it's going to / it'll probably rain tomorrow.

If there is strong evidence in the present situation, then we generally use going to:
I think it's going to rain this afternoon. (looking up at black clouds in the sky)

Going to or present continuous?

We use going to and the present continuous for plans and arrangements, and either is usually possible.
I'm going to have / I'm having a party on Friday.

  • Going to suggests that the details are not yet finalized — it's still just a plan.
    Jean is going to have another driving lesson soon. (a plan, with no specific time)
  • Present continuous suggests that the arrangement is more fixed, with a time and a place.
    Jean is having her driving lesson this afternoon. (it's fixed, in her diary)

Other meanings of will and shall

  • Is that the phone ringing? I'll answer it. (a spontaneous decision)
  • I'll do the best that I can to help you. (a promise)
  • Shall I help you? (offer/suggestion)

Escrita: Grammar · Reported Speech 1

When we tell someone what another person said, we use reported speech (or indirect speech). We often do this by using a reporting verb like say or tell, and we usually have to change the pronouns and the tense of the verb.

Tense changes

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told), the tense of the verb in the direct speech usually moves "one step back" into the past.

Present continuous → Past continuous

"I'm enjoying my new job." → She said she was enjoying her new job.

Present simple → Past simple

"My father isn't very well." → She said her father wasn't very well.

"We are going to buy a house." → They said they were going to buy a house.

Present perfect → Past perfect

"My sister has gone to Australia." → He said his sister had gone to Australia.

Can → Could

"I can't find a job." → She said she couldn't find a job.

Will → Would

"I'll phone you." → He said he would phone me.

Do/Does → Did

"My son doesn't like school." → She said her son didn't like school.

Look / Feel → Looked / Felt

"You look tired." → He said I looked tired.

"I feel fine." → I said I felt fine.

Say and Tell

We use say and tell to report what someone said, but they are used differently:

  • say (→ said) No direct object. You say something to someone. — He said that he was tired. (NOT He said me)
  • tell (→ told) Requires a direct object. You tell someone something. — He told me that he was tired. (NOT He told that...)
Watch out:
Do not say: he said me, I said Ann.
Do not say: he told to me, I told to Ann.

What did she say to you? (NOT say you)
What did she tell you? (NOT tell to you)

Using 'that'

You can choose to include or omit the word that. Both are correct:

  • He said that he was tired. OR He said he was tired.
  • Ann told me that she didn't like her job. OR Ann told me she didn't like her job.

Escrita: Grammar · Conditionals

Sentences with if where one event depends on another event are called conditionals. The if... clause is the condition and the other part of the sentence is the result.

0 If you arrive early, you get a better seat.

Zero conditional: talking generally about something that is always true.

1 If you arrive early, you'll get a better seat.

First conditional: describes what someone thinks will happen in a real situation. You believe that the things you are talking about will happen.

2 If you arrived early, you'd get a better seat.

Second conditional: describes what might happen in the future, or things you can imagine happening (imaginary condition).

3 If you had arrived early, you'd have got a better seat.

Third conditional: describes things you can imagine happening in the past. Impossible to change (see Grammar 13).

Zero conditional

Form: if + present simple, ... present simple

Used for things that are always or generally true. We can use when or if to introduce the sentence.

Example

It's a tropical country, so if it rains hard, everyone stays indoors.

It's a tropical country, so when it rains hard, everyone stays indoors.

We can use an imperative structure in the result clause. An imperative is when we tell people what to do.

  • If you feel dizzy, stop taking the tablets.
  • If you change your mind, give me a ring.

First conditional

Form: if + present simple, ... will/won't

Used for future events that will happen, or are likely to happen. The event is a real possibility in the speaker's mind, not imaginary.

  • If we walk so slowly, we'll be late.
  • If we run, we won't be late.
  • If we don't run, we will be late.
Other present tenses in the condition clause: It is possible to use other present tenses, not just the present simple.
If you're driving, I'll come with you. (present continuous)
If I've seen the film before, I'll let you know! (present perfect)

Second conditional

Form: if + past simple, ... would/wouldn't

Used for future events that are imaginary, unlikely or impossible.

  • If I had a helicopter, I'd fly to school.
  • If I flew to school, I wouldn't be late.

In speech and informal writing the short form of would is 'd.
I'd become famous. (= I would become famous.)

Time reference: A past simple tense form is used in the condition clause, but the time reference is the future.

Was/Were: We use the past forms of be in second conditionals (I was, you were, he was etc.), or we can use I were and he/she were in more formal sentences.
If I was/were an astronaut, I'd enjoy being weightless!

Unless

Unless means 'if not' in sentences where we say that if something does not happen, something else will happen. These are sentences which have the same meaning.

Compare

We'll go out for a walk if it doesn't rain.

We'll go out for a walk unless it rains.

Other uses of would

We use would in other situations which do not involve conditional sentences:

  • Reporting what someone said: He said he would call back later.
  • Polite offer: Would you like some more tea?
  • Polite request: Would you open the window, please?
  • Reporting a refusal: The boys wouldn't keep quiet.

Escrita: Describing People

A descriptive composition about a person should consist of three main parts: an introduction, a main body, and a conclusion. Such descriptions can be found in articles, letters, witness statements, novels, etc.

Structure of the composition

a) Introduction

Give some brief information about the person: his/her name, the time or place you met/saw him/her, or how you heard about him/her.

b) Main body

Describe physical appearance, personality characteristics, and hobbies/interests/everyday activities in separate paragraphs.

c) Conclusion

Include your comments and/or feelings about the person.

Points to consider

  • Topic sentences: Each paragraph starts with a topic sentence which introduces the subject of the paragraph. Use a variety of linking words to connect ideas.
  • Physical appearance: Give details moving from the most general aspects to the more specific details: height/build → age → facial features → hair → clothes.
    Example: John is a tall, slim man in his mid forties. He has a thin face, blue eyes and a large nose. His short hair is greying at the temples. He is usually casually dressed.
  • Character and behaviour: Support your description with examples.
    Example: Sally is very sociable. She loves going to parties and dances.
  • Negative qualities: Use mild language (tends to, seems to, is rather, can occasionally be, etc.).
    Instead of saying "Sally is arrogant", say "Sally tends to be rather arrogant".
  • Variety: Variety in the use of adjectives will make your description more interesting (e.g. good-natured, well-behaved, gorgeous, etc.).
  • Tenses: Use present tenses to describe someone connected to the present (e.g. someone you see every day). Use past tenses to describe someone related to the past (e.g. someone who is no longer alive or whom you won't meet again).

Paragraph Outline

Paragraph 1 Introduction — name, time met
Paragraph 2 Physical appearance
Paragraph 3 Personality characteristics + justification
Paragraph 4 Hobbies, interests, activities
Paragraph 5 Conclusion — comments & feelings

Vocabulary: Physical Appearance

Use these words to build your descriptions.

Height: tall, short, of medium height, just over six foot
Build: slim, thin, skinny, well-built, muscular, overweight, round-shouldered, long-legged
Age: teenaged, in his teens, early twenties, middle-aged, in his mid-forties, in his late forties, elderly
Complexion: pale, tanned, dark-skinned, fair
Face: round, oval, wrinkled, freckled, spotty
Hair: curly, wavy, straight, ginger, blond, balding, shoulder-length, thick
Eyes: almond-shaped, slanted, deep-set, bright, blue/green/brown
Nose: hooked, crooked, broad, flat
Lips: full, thin
Special features: mole, scar, dimples, beard, moustache

Manner and Mannerism

Instead of only using adjectives to describe a person's character, you can also include examples of the way they speak/look/smile to give a clearer picture and make the description more lively.

Example: His blue eyes light up whenever he sees his grandchild.
Although the adjectives kind and affectionate could be used, giving examples of mannerism makes the description more vivid.

Model Composition: "My Next-door Neighbour"

Notice how the writer moves from general to specific, uses present tenses, and provides examples for personality traits.

Mavis has been my neighbour for six years. I first met her when she knocked on my door and asked for a spade because she hadn't yet unpacked hers. She had only moved in two days before. (Introduction)

Physically, Mavis looks younger than most other women in their late sixties. She is of average height, neither fat nor thin. Her plump round face is framed by a mass of wavy white hair and her sparkling blue eyes show her humour and friendliness. She prefers wearing casual, comfortable clothes. I don't think I have ever seen her in a perfectly ironed suit. (Physical appearance)

As for her personality, Mavis's most striking characteristic is her generosity. My house, as well as most of our neighbours', nearly always has a vase of flowers from her garden in the living room. Mavis is very good-natured and always has time for a chat. She is also patient. She hardly ever gets annoyed about anything, except when children pick her favourite roses and lilies. However, she tends to be quite stubborn — once she has made a decision, nothing can change her mind. (Personality)

Mavis always seems to be busy doing something. She spends a lot of her time looking after her garden and often participates in local flower shows. When she is not in her garden she is usually off somewhere raising money for one charity or another. (Hobbies/Activities)

All in all, Mavis is the perfect next-door neighbour. Since the day she moved in, we have grown very close and I am very fond of her, even if I am woken up by the noise of her lawnmower early in the morning! (Conclusion)
